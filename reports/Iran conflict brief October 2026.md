# Iran war stalls between talks and strikes

**Bottom line (as of Oct 30, 2026):** For the past two weeks the US–Iran war has been stuck in what Iran's leaders call a state of **"neither war nor peace."** No US or Israeli strike on Iran was confirmed in this period. Fighting carried on at sea and on the Yemen–Saudi front, and mediators raced to get a deal before reported US plans to resume bombing after the **Nov 3 US midterm elections**. Iran kept attacking tankers in and near the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel that carries much of the Gulf's oil. Those attacks peaked with Iranian claims that four tankers hit mines on Oct 29–30. Meanwhile a US naval blockade held Iran's own oil exports at **about zero**. Qatar is carrying a draft deal between Washington and Tehran. Iran called one version "unacceptable" but is still talking, and an Iranian reply is expected around Oct 31. Oil stayed far above pre-war prices. Brent traded near **$116** and WTI near **$103** on Oct 30, and Brent was on course for an **~11% gain in October** ([BOE Report/Reuters](https://boereport.com/2026/10/29/oil-set-for-fourth-monthly-gain-as-hormuz-tanker-attack-highlights-supply-risks-from-war/)). The central dispute is about order. The US wants Iran's nuclear program on the table first. Iran wants the blockade and sanctions lifted first. Many battlefield claims below come from the warring parties themselves, and these are marked **[disputed]** or **[unverified]**.

*Background:* The war began on **Feb 28, 2026**, when US–Israeli strikes killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. His son Mojtaba succeeded him. A spring ceasefire and a June peace memorandum both broke down, and fighting resumed on **Jul 8** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Iran_war)). Since then the conflict has mostly been a contest of blockades. The US blocks Iran's ports, and Iran harasses shipping through Hormuz. A CENTCOM estimate puts the US cost at **about $47 billion** ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/live-updates/iran-war-donald-trump-qatar-mediators-new-proposal-end-war/)). Counts of US military deaths range from **18 to 23** depending on the source ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/29/pentagon-says-iran-war-has-cost-us-taxpayers-47bn-what-that-means); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/live-updates/iran-war-donald-trump-qatar-mediators-new-proposal-end-war/)).

Prices moved on headlines rather than on actual barrels. Talk of strikes and tanker attacks pushed them up, while news of diplomacy pulled them down. On Oct 29, for example, Brent fell about 1.7% after news of Vance's outreach ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/amp/2026/10/29/oil-price-today-wti-brent.html)). The spot price for oil available now ran **$8–13 above futures**, a sign that buyers are paying up for immediate cargoes. Physical supply is more mixed than prices suggest. Iran's seaborne exports are **at zero** under the blockade ([Iran International](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202610133210); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-19/iran-s-main-oil-terminals-remain-empty-as-us-blockade-bites)). Overall traffic through Hormuz has recovered to **roughly 70–82% of pre-war levels**, though estimates conflict ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/16/oil-price-today-iran-strait-hormuz-ukraine-russia.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Strait_of_Hormuz_crisis)). Shipping is very expensive: war-risk insurance runs **6–10% of a tanker's value per trip**, against about 0.25% before the war ([Boston Report](https://thebostonreport.com/2026/10/18/strait-of-hormuz-war-risk-insurance-rates-explained/)). Goldman Sachs estimates about **$25/barrel of "war premium"** in Brent and forecasts $90 by December ([investingLive](https://investinglive.com/commodities/goldman-sachs-sees-25-war-risk-premium-in-brent-forecasts-90-in-december-and-80-in-2027/)), while UBS sees about $110 ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/energy/2026/10/29/oil-prices-decline-after-diplomatic-push-in-iran-war/)).

The effects reach households and other markets. US diesel costs **$6.35 a gallon**, up from $3.75 a year ago ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/29/pentagon-says-iran-war-has-cost-us-taxpayers-47bn-what-that-means)), and US inflation rose to **3.7%** in September ([Inman](https://www.inman.com/2026/10/14/september-inflation-runs-hotter-than-expected-rising-3-7/)). Gold would normally rise in a war, but it has **fallen to about $3,900–4,000** from a January record near $5,600. Higher interest rates and a stronger dollar have outweighed its appeal as a safe haven ([investingLive](https://investinglive.com/commodities/gold-heads-for-its-biggest-yearly-drop-in-11-years-as-the-iran-war-lifts-yields-and-the-dollar/)).

## Three things to watch

**1. Iran's answer to Qatar (around Oct 31).** CBS reports "signs of progress," and direct US–Iran talks could begin as soon as next week in Qatar or Turkey ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/live-updates/iran-war-donald-trump-qatar-mediators-new-proposal-end-war/)). The test is whether either side gives ground on which steps come first.

**2. What Washington does after Nov 3.** The reported plan for a "brief but intense" campaign has no final decision behind it, according to AP ([The New Arab](https://www.newarab.com/news/no-majority-israeli-election-trump-mulls-next-steps-iran)). The IAEA's inability to account for Iran's enriched uranium is the most likely stated justification if strikes resume.

**3. Attacks on shipping and Saudi oil sites.** More tanker strikes in Hormuz, or a Houthi hit on Saudi export routes through the Red Sea, would hit a market with little cushion left. The US completed its 172-million-barrel reserve release on Oct 29 ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov/hgeo/opr/articles/energy-department-awards-contracts-strategic-petroleum-reserve-part-president)), and OPEC+ meets Nov 1 ([Quartz](https://qz.com/opec-plus-november-oil-output-targets-iran-war-100526)).

## Conclusion

Most of the oil has come back, but the emergency buffers are gone. Gulf supply has largely returned through bypass routes, so today's roughly $115 Brent mostly prices the *risk* of renewed war rather than a measured shortage. That makes the next week's choice between a Qatari-brokered deal and a post-election strike campaign close to an either/or outcome for prices. A deal could take much of Goldman's estimated $25 premium out quickly. Strikes would hit a market whose strategic reserves are largely spent. Because the war's main financial effect runs through inflation and interest rates, escalation would likely push up borrowing costs as much as fuel prices.
